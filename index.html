El Cajon first

An appraiser who works the valley street by street

El Cajon is a bowl, and the bowl matters. Homes on the flat valley floor around Main Street, Broadway and Magnolia Avenue trade on a different footing than houses climbing the rim toward Fletcher Hills, Granite Hills or the slopes below Mount Helix, where a canyon view or a bigger lot can shift value by a wide margin. Much of the city was built in a rush after World War II, so whole streets share the same 1950s and 1960s ranch plans, and the differences come down to what owners added later: enclosed patios, garage conversions, second units and room additions that may or may not carry permits. Then there are the edges. Unincorporated pockets such as Bostonia, Crest and Rancho San Diego sit right against city limits, with county rules, septic in places and higher fire exposure. An appraiser who works these streets knows which sales actually compare.

On El Cajon streets

Four things that move a value here

01

Floor or rim

Elevation does a lot of the work in Cajon Valley pricing. A house on a quarter-acre hillside lot off Fletcher Parkway or in Granite Hills can carry a view, more privacy and cooler evenings, while a similar plan on the valley floor near Second Street trades on access and convenience. Any comparable sale must sit on the same side of that boundary.

02

Postwar tract plans

Large stretches of El Cajon were subdivided between roughly 1950 and 1970, so many homes started as near-identical three-bedroom ranches. Because the base plans match, the adjustments that decide value are condition, updating, square footage added since, and lot shape. Original single-pane windows, galvanized plumbing and tired roofs show up often in older estates.

03

Conversions and second units

Converted garages, enclosed patios and detached units are everywhere in the valley, and their permit history changes how they are treated. A permitted accessory dwelling can add measurable value and rental use. An unpermitted bedroom carved from a garage usually gets far less credit, and the report has to say clearly which is which.

04

Noise, traffic and edges

Gillespie Field sits on the city's northwest side, and Interstate 8, State Route 67 and State Route 125 frame the valley. Homes under the flight path or backing onto a freeway sound wall often sell at a discount. On the outer rim, county jurisdiction, fire hazard mapping and septic systems become part of the analysis.

Ordering

From request to signed report

  1. Send a request

    Begin by sharing the address, why the appraisal is needed and the effective date the value must reflect. A few lines through the request form are enough. Mention any deadline, court date or filing window so the schedule can be planned around it.

  2. Review a written quote

    Brian replies with a written quote describing the report type that fits the purpose, the expected delivery date and the fee. Nothing starts until the client approves it, and questions about scope are answered before anyone commits.

  3. Research and site visit

    Brian pulls public records, permit history and comparable sales for the effective date. When the assignment includes a visit, he measures the home, photographs each room and notes condition, additions and anything that affects marketability.

  4. Signed report delivered

    The finished appraisal arrives as a signed PDF that can be sent to an attorney, filed with a court or attached to a tax form. Brian remains available afterward to explain the conclusions or answer follow-up questions from the people relying on it.

Q&A

Before you order

Where does Brian inspect homes in person?

Inspected work (Drive-By and Standard reports, including 2-4 unit Standards) happens only in the 68 communities Brian Ward Appraisal covers in person: 33 in San Diego County, El Cajon included, and 35 in Riverside County. A property outside that list, whether in the San Diego backcountry, Los Angeles or the Bay Area, is handled with a desktop report. Each town page on this site says which applies.

Why does the order form ask about an effective date?

Many private-party assignments need the value as of a specific past day: the date of death for an estate, the petition date in a bankruptcy, a separation date in a divorce. The form asks whether a particular date applies and, if so, which one, so the quote reflects the right research period from the start. If nobody is sure yet, choose Not sure and Brian will help sort it out.

How long does an appraisal take?

The written quote for an El Cajon home states a firm delivery date before any work begins, and Brian plans the schedule around it. Retrospective dates, large rural parcels and properties with unusual improvements can take longer because the comparable sales are harder to locate and verify. If a hearing or filing is scheduled, mention it with the request so the delivery date can be set around that deadline.

Is Brian able to value a home as of an earlier date?

Yes. A big portion of his work is retrospective, most often for estates needing a stepped-up basis at the date of death and for divorces keyed to the date of separation. The analysis uses sales that closed around that earlier date, along with records and family recollections about how the home looked at the time. Dates going back many years are possible when enough information survives.

What is a desktop appraisal?

A desktop appraisal is prepared without visiting the home. Brian works from county records, permit files, prior listing photos, aerial imagery and photos or notes the owner provides, then applies the same sales comparison analysis used in any other report. It is still a signed appraisal by a certified appraiser. It works best when the property's condition is clear from the available information and no party is likely to challenge it.

Will a court accept the report?

Brian prepares his reports to the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice, and a California certified residential appraiser signs each one, which matches what courts handling probate, family and civil matters generally expect. Whether a particular judge gives weight to a report depends on the case, so attorneys sometimes ask for an inspected report or added detail in contested matters. Brian can discuss those needs before the quote is written.
