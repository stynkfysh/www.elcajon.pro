About City Heights

City Heights covers a broad stretch of Mid-City between Interstate 805 and the College Area, with El Cajon Boulevard and University Avenue as its main corridors. Incorporated as East San Diego in 1912, it voted to merge with San Diego in 1923. Canyons such as Swan Canyon, Fox Canyon and the branches of Chollas Creek split it into many small neighborhoods. Teralta Park sits on a freeway lid over Interstate 15, one of the more unusual parks in the region.

Housing you find here

Bungalows from the 1910s through the 1940s fill much of the area, with Spanish Revival cottages in places such as Islenair, which has its own historic district. Azalea Park has hillside homes on canyon lots, some with artist-built additions and terraced gardens. Stucco houses from after the war and plenty of small apartment buildings are found across the area. Lots are often deep enough for second units, and companion units built over decades are widespread, some permitted and many not. Corner lots near University Avenue often hold small storefronts with flats above.

Value levers in City Heights

Prices vary block by block depending on canyon views, lot size, traffic and condition. Homes on quiet canyon-edge streets in Azalea Park or Islenair trade higher than those on busy corridors. Transit access along the Rapid bus lines supports demand. Lots zoned for more density hold land value, and ADU-friendly rules have encouraged backyard construction. Chollas Creek proximity can bring flood zone issues.

Notes for an appraisal here

Brian checks permit records carefully, because unpermitted second units and additions are common and legal unit count changes the valuation approach. Flood mapping is reviewed for properties near Chollas Creek channels. Comparables need to come from the same sub-neighborhood when possible, because Azalea Park, Teralta and Fairmount Park sell differently. Canyon lots get fire and slope review, and freeway-adjacent parcels near I-15 and I-805 are adjusted for noise.

The family home in City Heights has an extra unit in back built by grandpa. How is that handled?

Brian first checks whether the unit was permitted. A legal second unit can be valued for its income and space. If it was never permitted, it may still contribute some value, but buyers discount for the risk and cost of legalizing it. The report states which applies so heirs or an attorney can plan around it.
