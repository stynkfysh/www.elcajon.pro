About Downtown San Diego

Downtown San Diego occupies the low ground between San Diego Bay and the slopes of Balboa Park, bounded roughly by Interstate 5 on the north and east. Under the city's Centre City plan, the area is split into the Gaslamp Quarter, East Village, Cortez Hill, the Marina, Columbia, the Core and Little Italy. Since Petco Park opened in 2004, East Village has changed from warehouses and rail yards to a dense residential district, and towers now line the waterfront along Harbor Drive and Pacific Highway.

Housing you find here

Nearly all owner-occupied housing here is condominium. Low- and mid-rise complexes built in the 1980s and 1990s fill the Marina district. Most towers in East Village, Columbia and Cortez Hill were finished from the early 2000s onward, with a second wave after 2015. Loft buildings in the Gaslamp and warehouse conversions near the ballpark add exposed-brick units. Some projects include deed-restricted affordable units with resale caps. Single-family houses are essentially absent, although a few older row houses and cottages survive on Cortez Hill.

Value levers in Downtown San Diego

Floor height and view direction drive price more than square footage alone. A unit facing the bay or Coronado sells at a clear premium over one facing a parking structure or an adjoining tower, and new construction can erase a view that existed a few years earlier. HOA dues, the number of deeded parking spaces, building age and amenities all matter. Noise from the trolley, freight trains, ballpark events and aircraft on final approach affects specific stacks.

Notes for an appraisal here

The best comparables are sales in the same building, ideally the same floor plan stack, because tower-to-tower differences in dues and finish are large. Brian reads HOA records for special assessments, pending construction-defect litigation and rental or short-term rental restrictions. Resale-restricted affordable units must be valued under their covenant, not at market. On a retrospective date for an estate or Prop 19 filing, he confirms what was built around the unit at that time, since later towers may have changed the view.

Dad's condo is in a downtown tower. Why does the appraiser care which way the windows face?

Within a tower, identical floor plans can sell at very different prices because of the view. A bay-facing unit on a high floor competes in a different price tier than one looking into the next building. Brian identifies the exact orientation and floor, then leans on sales from that same stack or a matching one so the conclusion reflects what buyers actually pay for that outlook.
