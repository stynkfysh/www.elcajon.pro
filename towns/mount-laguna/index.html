About Mount Laguna

Mount Laguna is a small mountain village inside the Laguna Mountain Recreation Area of Cleveland National Forest, sitting near 6,000 feet on Sunrise Highway (County Route S1). Jeffrey pines and black oaks give way to meadows, and the eastern rim drops sharply toward the Anza-Borrego Desert. The Laguna Mountain Lodge, a small store, campgrounds and SDSU's Mount Laguna Observatory form the core. Winter can bring heavy snow, and summer visitors arrive for the Pacific Crest Trail and cooler air.

Housing you find here

Most homes on Mount Laguna are cabins built under Forest Service recreation residence permits. The owner holds the structure while the land itself remains federal, under a special use permit renewed with the Forest Service. Cabins range from rustic one-room structures from the 1920s and 1930s to more substantial year-round homes. A handful of privately owned parcels exist nearby, and the 2013 Chariot Fire burned structures on the mountain, so some sites carry rebuilt or vacant permits.

Value levers in Mount Laguna

Value is closely tied to the permit terms, since buyers acquire a cabin on land they do not own. Annual permit charges, Forest Service rules on alterations and limits on full-time occupancy all affect demand. Desert-rim views and proximity to the lodge add appeal. Snow access, the long drive from town, and fire exposure restrain prices. Privately owned parcels nearby usually sell for considerably more than comparable permit cabins.

Notes for an appraisal here

The first task is to establish whether the property is a permit cabin or privately owned land, since the two are valued differently. The permit, its annual charges and any compliance notices should be reviewed. Only other permit cabin sales on Mount Laguna or similar tracts make valid comparables for permit properties. Rebuild status after the Chariot Fire, water supply arrangements and access in winter should all be documented. Sales are infrequent and seasonal.

My grandfather's Mount Laguna cabin is on Forest Service land. How is that valued for his trust?

Brian values the cabin improvements and the permit interest, not the land, because the land belongs to the national forest. He looks for sales of other Laguna recreation residence cabins and reviews the permit terms and charges that a buyer would assume. The report spells out that the valuation excludes ownership of the land itself so the trustee understands exactly what was appraised.
