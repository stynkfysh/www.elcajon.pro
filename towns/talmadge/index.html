About Talmadge

Talmadge lies east of Kensington between El Cajon Boulevard and the canyons above Interstate 8. Its main subdivision, Talmadge Park, opened in the mid-1920s and was named for the Talmadge sisters, silent-film actresses who were among its promoters. Decorative gates at several entrances and ornamental streetlights recall that period, and the community has restored many of them. Euclid Avenue, Monroe Avenue and Fairmount Avenue are key streets, and El Cajon Boulevard forms the southern commercial edge.

Housing you find here

Spanish Revival and Mediterranean homes from the 1920s and 1930s fill the original tract, with Craftsman, Tudor and Monterey styles scattered among them. Postwar ranch houses occupy parcels developed after the war. Lots are typically larger than in North Park or City Heights, and many have detached garages and room for additions or a backyard unit. Canyon-edge streets on the north and west carry views toward Mission Valley. Small apartment buildings line El Cajon Boulevard along the southern edge.

Value levers in Talmadge

Original architecture, lot size and canyon views account for most price differences. The restored gated entrances and period lampposts give the area an identity that buyers recognize and pay for. Homes close to El Cajon Boulevard draw less than those deep in the tract. Canyon lots bring brush management obligations and some slope concerns. Proximity to SDSU adds rental demand on the eastern side, and investor interest there can push prices on smaller homes.

Notes for an appraisal here

Brian distinguishes homes in the original Talmadge Park tract from surrounding parcels, since buyers price them differently. Older homes need checks for original electrical, galvanized plumbing, raised foundations and additions completed without permits. Canyon properties are reviewed for slope and fire exposure. Comparables come from Talmadge or Kensington first. When an effective date lies years back, he rebuilds the market as it stood then rather than adjusting current sales.

Is a Talmadge Park home inside the original tract worth more than one a few blocks away?

Often, yes. The original 1920s tract carries an identity that buyers recognize, with the gates, streetlights and period architecture. Homes outside it may be similar in size but sell differently. Brian compares the home first with sales inside the same tract and then adjusts if he needs to look further, explaining any location adjustment in the report.
