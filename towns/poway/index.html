About Poway

Poway sits in a broad inland valley east of Interstate 15, ringed by granite peaks such as Iron Mountain, Mount Woodson and the Twin Peaks ridge. The city incorporated in 1980 and has kept a rural zoning pattern ever since, which is why horse trails, citrus rows and large lots sit only minutes from Poway Road's commercial strip. The northern hills hold Lake Poway and the Blue Sky Ecological Reserve, and industry is concentrated in the South Poway business park along Scripps Poway Parkway.

Housing you find here

Poway's older core around Midland Road and Poway Road holds 1950s and 1960s ranch houses on modest lots. Tract construction picked up during the 1970s and 1980s, adding one- and two-story stucco houses in neighborhoods off Twin Peaks Road and Pomerado Road. North of the valley floor, Green Valley and the Garden Road area carry custom homes on acre-plus parcels, many zoned for horses. Old Coach Golf Estates adds newer view estates along the northern edge, and condos and mobile home parks fill pockets near Poway Road.

Value levers in Poway

Lot size and usable flat land carry real weight in Poway, since an acre with corrals or a riding arena trades differently than a steep acre that is mostly brush. Mountain and valley views matter on ridge lots. Homes backing to Poway Road, Scripps Poway Parkway or SR-67 hear steady traffic noise. Insurance costs and buyer interest are shaped by the very high fire hazard zones across the north and east hills, which lost homes in the 2003 Cedar Fire and 2007 Witch Fire.

Notes for an appraisal here

Many rural Poway lots run on septic systems, and some still have private wells, so the appraiser confirms how a home is served rather than assuming a sewer connection. Barns, guest quarters and converted garages are common, and their permit status changes how they are counted. Rebuilt homes in burn areas may be much newer than the neighbors. For retrospective date-of-death work, equestrian estates often require comparables from Ramona or the Rancho Bernardo hills because matching sales inside Poway are thin.

Does a horse property in Poway get valued like a regular house?

Not quite. Brian looks at the land first: how much of the parcel is usable, whether the zoning allows animals, and what improvements such as barns, arenas and fencing actually add. Then he searches for sales of other equestrian parcels, which sometimes means reaching into Ramona or the Rancho Bernardo hills. A standard Poway tract sale rarely captures what a buyer pays for a working horse setup.
