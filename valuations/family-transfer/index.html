Family Transfer Appraisal

Selling a house to a son, gifting a share to a daughter, or buying out a sibling's interest all raise the same question: what is the home worth? Brian Ward prepares independent appraisals for transfers between relatives so the number can stand up to questions from the IRS, the county assessor or other family members. A documented fair market value also helps keep relationships intact when one person benefits from the deal.

Gifts and partial gifts

When a parent sells a house to a child for less than market value, the difference may be treated as a gift. Federal gift tax rules involve annual exclusions, lifetime exemptions and reporting thresholds that change over time. A supportable fair market value is the starting point for any of those calculations. Brian provides that value as of the transfer date, documented with sales and a clear explanation. He does not determine whether a gift tax return is needed or how a transfer should be structured. The family's CPA or estate planning attorney handles that, using the appraisal as one piece of the planning file.

Keeping the peace among siblings

Parents who help one child into a home sometimes create tension with the others, especially if the price looks generous. An independent appraisal shows everyone where the starting point was. Later, if the parents want to equalize inheritances, the documented value makes that easier. Brian writes reports in plain language so a relative who is not involved in real estate can follow the reasoning. He does not take sides, and the report does not recommend a price. It simply states what the open market would likely pay, which gives the family a neutral reference for whatever arrangement they choose. That clarity tends to help most years later.

Reassessment and Prop 19

Transfers between parents and children can trigger property tax reassessment, and Proposition 19 rules determine when part or all of the existing taxable value may carry over. The child generally must use the family home as a principal residence, and the exclusion has a value cap tied to the factored base-year value plus an inflation-adjusted one million dollars. Market value at transfer therefore matters for the tax calculation. Brian supplies that value. Filing claims with the assessor and deciding whether a transfer qualifies are tasks for the family's attorney or tax advisor, who know the current rules and deadlines. Missing a filing window can be costly.

Buying out a co-owner

Relatives often co-own property: siblings who inherited together, a parent and adult child on title, or cousins holding a rental. When one wants out, the buyout needs a fair number. Brian can appraise the whole property, and when asked, discuss how partial interests are sometimes valued differently in the market, though that analysis is specialized and the attorney should confirm whether it applies. Most families simply want the full market value so they can divide it by ownership percentages. A transfer likely to draw questions from the IRS or other heirs often warrants an inspected report; a simpler one may be handled with a desktop report.

In El Cajon and East County

Homes on larger lots in Granite Hills, Bostonia and the unincorporated areas near Lakeside often have a second unit or a converted garage where a parent or adult child lives. Those layouts complicate a family transfer because the extra unit may or may not be permitted. Brian sorts out how buyers in the valley actually treat these setups, and whether a property on septic in Crest or Harbison Canyon faces limits on additional living space.

Common questions

Does a family sale at full price still need an appraisal?

It can help. Even when relatives agree on a price, an independent appraisal confirms that the figure reflects the market, which protects both sides if the IRS, the assessor or another relative later asks questions. A tax professional can say whether documentation is advisable for a specific transfer, but many families find the record worth having.

Can the appraisal be dated to a transfer that already happened?

Yes. Retrospective appraisals are common for family transfers recorded months or years earlier, particularly when a tax preparer requests support after the fact. Brian sets the effective date to the transfer date and uses sales from that period. Older photos, receipts for work done before the transfer, and notes from the people involved all help reconstruct the home's condition at that time.

Will the appraiser help decide the sale price?

Brian provides the market value opinion. The family decides the actual price, and that decision may involve gift planning, fairness among heirs and personal priorities. A CPA or estate attorney can explain the tax effects of selling below market so the family makes an informed choice. Brian's report keeps the market figure separate from those personal decisions.
