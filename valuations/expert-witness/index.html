Expert Witness and Litigation Appraisal

Real estate disputes often come down to a contested number. Partition actions, partnership breakups, construction defect claims, boundary fights and fraud cases all depend on what a property was worth at a specific time. Brian Ward's litigation appraisals are built to withstand opposing counsel's reading and cross-examination, and he can act as an expert witness if an attorney decides testimony would benefit the case.

Reports built for scrutiny

A litigation appraisal will be read by someone looking for weaknesses. Every comparable, adjustment and assumption must have a reason that can be explained aloud. Brian writes these reports with that reader in mind, documenting the data sources, the verification steps and the logic connecting the evidence to the conclusion. Where judgment is involved, the report says so and explains the basis for it. This approach takes more time than a routine assignment, but it produces work that can withstand deposition questioning. Attorneys benefit from understanding the analysis early, so they can decide how it fits their strategy and spot problems before opposing experts do.

Deposition and trial testimony

With more than two decades of appraisal practice and thousands of assignments, Brian can speak to valuation methods in terms a judge or jury can follow. Testimony is arranged separately from the written report and depends on the attorney's needs. Brian prepares by reviewing the file, the opposing appraisal if one exists, and the questions likely to arise. He testifies to his opinion and the reasoning behind it, not to legal conclusions. Decisions about whether to designate an expert, how to present the testimony, and how it fits procedural rules remain with the attorney handling the case. Brian's job is to be clear and candid on the stand.

Reviewing another appraiser's work

At times the most helpful service is reviewing the other side's appraisal. A review examines whether the other report's data, adjustments and reasoning support its conclusion, and identifies errors or unsupported assumptions. Brian can perform a review with or without offering his own value opinion, depending on what the attorney requests. Reviews are often a cost-effective first step: if the opposing report holds up, the client may choose to settle. If it has clear flaws, the review provides a roadmap for cross-examination and may justify a full independent appraisal. Counsel chooses the scope once the initial findings are discussed.

Disputes that commonly need values

Partition actions between co-owners, dissolutions of business partnerships that hold real estate, disputes over a seller's failure to disclose, construction defect cases, easement and encroachment conflicts, and claims of elder financial abuse involving a home transfer all tend to require independent valuation. Many involve retrospective dates, or a before-and-after comparison to measure damages. Brian works on residential property and focuses on the valuation question; the legal theory belongs to counsel. Where the case stands, and how close discovery deadlines are, usually determines whether the engagement opens with a consultation, a review, a desktop report or an inspected report. Brian is comfortable stepping in at any of those stages.

In El Cajon and East County

Civil and family matters for East County are frequently heard at the Superior Court's East County Regional Center in El Cajon, and local disputes over property bring their own complications. Hillside parcels on Mount Helix and in Granite Hills can involve easement and drainage conflicts, older lots near downtown sometimes have encroaching structures, and inherited houses shared by siblings lead to partition claims. Brian has the local familiarity to explain how those specific issues affect value in testimony that a judge can follow.

Common questions

Can the appraiser consult before being designated as an expert?

Yes. Many attorneys begin with a consulting arrangement to understand the valuation issues before deciding whether to designate an expert. That lets counsel test the strength of the position privately. Whether consulting work is protected from discovery and how designation should proceed are legal questions for the attorney, who controls the engagement.

Does the appraiser need to see the property?

Not always. Some disputes involve properties that have since been altered, sold or demolished, and the appraisal relies on records and photos. When the property is accessible and its present condition matters, an interior visit usually strengthens the report. Brian will recommend a scope that fits the question being asked and explain the trade-offs.

What areas of real estate does Brian testify about?

His work focuses on residential property in San Diego County, including single-family homes, condos, small multi-unit buildings and residential land. He testifies about market value and the methods used to reach it. Questions outside that scope, such as commercial property or construction costs, may call for a different expert, and he will say so.
