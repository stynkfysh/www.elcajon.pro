Divorce Appraisal

Dividing a marriage usually means deciding what the house is worth, and few numbers carry more emotion. Brian Ward provides neutral appraisals for spouses, their attorneys and mediators, with the effective date set to whatever the case requires, often the date of separation or a current date near trial. The analysis sticks to sales evidence and property facts, which gives both sides a common reference point instead of two competing guesses.

Picking the right effective date

A divorce appraisal is only useful if it answers the right question, and the date is part of that question. Some cases call for the value at separation. Others need a current figure because one spouse plans to buy out the other, or the house will be sold and the proceeds split. Occasionally both dates matter. Brian confirms the effective date with the attorney or mediator before starting, then researches the sales that best reflect the market on that day. Getting this settled at the outset avoids paying for a second report later, and it prevents the appraisal from being set aside because it measured the wrong moment.

Staying neutral when tension is high

Brian does not advocate for either spouse. He may be hired by one side, by both jointly, or through a mediator, but the opinion follows the evidence wherever it leads. During any property visit he speaks with whoever provides access, notes the condition, and avoids discussing the case itself. Comments from either party about what the house should be worth do not shape the result. That posture matters most when one spouse still lives in the home and the other has moved out, since each may describe its condition very differently. The report records what was observed and what was reported, and keeps those two categories clearly separated.

Improvements, damage and separate property

Couples often argue about who paid for the new roof, who let the pool go green, or whether a garage conversion added value. The appraiser's job is more limited than those disputes: to describe the home and to estimate the price the market would have paid for it in its condition on the effective date. If an attorney needs to know how much a specific improvement contributed, Brian can sometimes address that with a separate analysis, but questions of separate versus community property and reimbursement claims are legal matters. Those belong with the family law attorney, who decides how the appraisal figures fit into the overall division.

What attorneys and mediators receive

The finished report states its intended users, effective date, scope of work, and the comparable sales relied on, with a written explanation of each adjustment. Mediators appreciate a document that both spouses can read without technical training. Attorneys appreciate one that can be shared with opposing counsel without leaving obvious gaps to attack. High-stakes cases often justify an inspected report, while a quick settlement conversation may need only a desktop report, and Brian talks that through with whoever orders the work. When the case moves toward trial, the same appraisal can serve as a foundation for testimony, if the attorney decides that is needed.

In El Cajon and East County

Family law matters for East County residents are commonly heard at the San Diego Superior Court's East County Regional Center on East Main Street in El Cajon, so local attorneys see a steady stream of house valuations. The homes involved vary widely: 1950s tract houses off Washington Avenue, condos along Broadway, hillside places on Mount Helix with long views, and larger lots in Rancho San Diego. Each draws a different buyer pool, which is why a valley-wide average does a poor job in a divorce settlement.

  • The effective date requested, such as separation or a current date
  • Names and contact details of both attorneys or the mediator
  • Who will provide access to the home, if a visit is planned
  • List of major improvements and roughly when they were done
  • Any prior appraisals or broker opinions in the case file
  • Case number, if the report must reference it

Common questions

Can spouses share one appraisal instead of hiring two?

Yes, and many couples do. A jointly ordered appraisal names both parties as intended users and often reduces conflict, since neither side can claim the appraiser was chosen to favor the other. Each attorney should agree on the effective date and scope before the work starts. If one side still disagrees with the result, they remain free to obtain a review or second opinion.

What if one spouse refuses to allow access?

Even so, an appraisal can be done with public records, earlier listing photos and an exterior look, though the report will note the interior was not seen and explain the assumptions. Attorneys sometimes seek a court order for access. Brian will describe the limitation honestly so that the reader understands how it may affect reliability.

Will the appraiser testify if the case goes to trial?

Brian can provide testimony when an attorney requests it, and that work is arranged separately from the original report. Plenty of divorce cases settle after both parties see a credible figure, so testimony is not required in every case. The attorney handling the case is the right person to decide whether expert testimony fits the overall strategy.
