Pre-Purchase Appraisal

Buyers often rely on the listing agent's pricing, a few online estimates and their own instincts. A pre-purchase appraisal adds a fourth perspective from someone with no commission at stake. Brian Ward reviews the specific house, the closest sales and current competition, then offers an independent opinion of market value that helps a buyer decide how much to offer, when to walk away, or which issues deserve another look.

A second opinion with no commission

Agents on both sides of a deal are paid when it closes, and even ethical professionals feel that pull. Brian has no financial stake in whether the purchase happens or at what price. That independence lets him point out a weak comparable in the listing packet, a sale that was really a distressed transfer, or a pricing assumption that does not fit the street. Buyers paying cash, buying from a relative, or purchasing in an unusual part of the county find this especially useful, since no third party is otherwise reviewing the price. The report gives them a reasoned figure to bring back to their agent before writing an offer.

Unusual properties need closer study

Tract homes with many recent sales are relatively easy to price. Properties with acreage, a converted garage, a granny flat, a septic system, or a steep driveway are harder, and that is where buyers most often overpay. Brian looks at how the market has actually treated similar features rather than assuming each one adds value. An unpermitted addition, for instance, may contribute far less than the square footage suggests. A home backing a busy road may sell at a discount even when the interior is excellent. Laying out those effects before the contract is signed gives the buyer leverage and a clearer sense of risk.

Timing within the escrow

Pre-purchase work moves on a buyer's calendar, which is often tight. Some buyers order an appraisal before making an offer on a property they have been watching. Others get one while the contingency period is open to back up a request to lower the price. Brian talks through the timeline at the start so the report arrives while it can still change the outcome. When the seller will not allow a visit before an offer, a desktop report can still be prepared; with access, an inspected report adds detail. Buyers should check their purchase contract for contingency dates and ask their agent or attorney how an appraisal result can be used in negotiation.

What the appraisal does not replace

An appraisal estimates market value. It is not a home inspection, a pest report, a survey or a title review. Brian notes visible condition issues because they affect value, but he does not test systems, crawl attics, or certify that anything works. Buyers should still hire a licensed home inspector and review the seller's disclosures carefully. Likewise, questions about contract terms, contingency removal or legal remedies go to the buyer's agent or a real estate attorney. Used alongside those other reports, an independent value opinion helps a buyer see the whole picture before committing, which is the point of ordering one.

In El Cajon and East County

Buyers shopping in El Cajon face a valley with very different submarkets in a small area. A 1950s tract house near Broadway, a newer planned development on the east side, a Granite Hills home on a half-acre, and a Crest property on septic with brush to clear can all list near one another in price yet appeal to different buyers. Homes under the Gillespie Field approach paths or close to Interstate 8 and State Route 67 deserve their own scrutiny, and Brian checks how those locations actually sell.

Common questions

Can an appraisal be ordered before making an offer?

Yes. Some buyers order one on a property they are seriously considering, especially when the price looks aggressive or few comparable sales exist. Access to the interior may be limited before an offer is accepted, so the report will state what was viewed and what was assumed. That still gives the buyer an independent figure to bring to negotiations.

Does the seller need to agree to the appraisal?

An appraisal based on public records and exterior observation does not require seller consent. An interior visit does require permission, which is normally arranged through the agents. Brian can work either way and will explain how the level of access affects the reliability of the conclusion. Buyers sometimes start with exterior-only work and add an interior visit once an offer is accepted.

What happens if the appraised value is lower than the contract price?

That information gives the buyer something concrete to discuss with the seller, such as a price reduction or a credit, if the contract still allows it. Whether to renegotiate, cancel or proceed is the buyer's decision, made with their agent or attorney. The report simply provides the market evidence behind the conversation.
