Garage Conversions, ADUs and Permit History in El Cajon Homes

Decades of owner-built additions, converted garages and newer backyard units make permit research one of the most important steps in valuing an El Cajon house for an estate, trust, divorce or family transfer.

Why so many El Cajon houses have grown

The postwar building boom left El Cajon with thousands of modest single-story homes, many under 1,400 square feet, set on lots with room to spare. Over sixty or seventy years, owners filled that room. Single-car and two-car garages became bedrooms, dens or rental quarters. Covered patios were enclosed. Rear additions, family rooms and occasional second stories followed as households grew. Some of this work went through the city's permit and approval process; a great deal did not, particularly in earlier decades when enforcement was lighter and owners did the work themselves. The result is a housing stock where the house described in public records can differ noticeably from the house standing on the lot. For an appraiser, that gap is not a side issue. It determines how much space is counted as living area, which comparables fit, and how confident any reader can be in the final opinion of value.

Checking records before counting square footage

The starting point is the county assessor's characteristics: recorded square footage, bedroom and bath counts and year built. Brian compares those figures with the floor plan, measurements and photographs available for the assignment, and with any permits or final approvals the owner, heirs or attorney can provide. When a discrepancy appears, the next step is the City of El Cajon's building records, or the county's if the parcel sits in unincorporated territory with an El Cajon mailing address. A permitted, well-built conversion that meets code is generally treated as gross living area. Space that lacks permits, uses substandard construction or has unusual access is usually reported separately, with any contribution to value supported by what the market actually pays. Clear labeling in the report matters, because attorneys, courts and tax reviewers need to see exactly what was counted and why.

ADUs under California's newer rules

California has steadily expanded the right to build accessory dwelling units and junior ADUs, and El Cajon lots, often wide and level, are well suited to them. Detached backyard cottages, garage-to-ADU conversions and attached units have all become more common across the valley. A legally permitted ADU can add meaningful value, both through the extra living space and through potential rental income, and the appraiser may develop an income-based cross-check where the data supports one. Still, buyers do not pay the same premium everywhere: unit size, quality, separate entry, parking and utility metering all influence how much a second unit contributes. An unpermitted back house is a different matter. Some value may remain, but it accounts for the expense and risk of either legalizing it or removing it. Brian identifies which situation applies and avoids treating an unapproved structure as a legal second dwelling.

Lost garages, parking and retrospective dates

Converting a garage creates space but removes covered parking, and in parts of El Cajon where street parking is tight, buyers notice. The appraiser weighs the added room against the missing garage rather than assuming one simply replaces the other. Driveway length, carport additions and space for an RV or boat also enter that analysis. Retrospective assignments add another layer. When the effective date is a date of death, the date of separation or the date of a Prop 19 transfer, the issue becomes what was actually present at that time. An ADU completed afterward, or a conversion added by a new owner, is excluded; an addition that existed then is included even if it was later removed. Building permits, dated photographs, listing records and family recollections help establish the timeline, and the report states which sources were relied upon.
